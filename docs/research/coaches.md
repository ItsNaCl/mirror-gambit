# How the best known coaches teach club-level and beginner players

Paraphrased from public sources, October 2026. Confidence varies: Heisman, Silman, Aagaard, Chess Dojo, Chessly's course structure and Naroditsky (through fan compiled lists) are well documented. Nakamura, Carlsen, Finegold and Bartholomew teach mostly in video, so the written record is thin. The Chessbrah Building Habits list could not be confirmed from a primary source.

## Coach by coach

### Levy Rozman (GothamChess), How to Win at Chess and Chessly
- Structure: the book has two halves, roughly 0 to 800 and 800 to 1200, each covering openings, endgames, tactics, strategy in that order. About half the pages are diagrams and each chapter links to drills on Chessly. The Chessly beginner course has 13 chapters from basics through model games, each a short video, then an interactive study, quizzes and drills.
- Under 1200: rules and checkmate patterns first, then one opening per colour, then thinking habits, pawn and piece use, basic tactics.
- Openings come first in his approach. Most of Chessly's catalogue is opening repertoires.
- Move choice: scan checks, captures and attacks.
- Why it works: short, visual, entertaining, followed by drills. A reviewer's criticism: describes and shows more than it explains.

### Daniel Naroditsky (speedruns)
- Format: a fresh account climbed from low rating with the thinking narrated live, often one opening. Mistakes of his own and good moves by opponents pointed out.
- Move choice and plans: the wishlist method (say what you want, then find the move); give the job to the least valuable piece that can do it; do not react to every opponent move unless the threat is real; fix the cause of a tactic, not the symptom; keep plans to about three moves; prefer temporary weaknesses over permanent ones; ask "what if I play it anyway"; before two moves in a row, check whether the second works first; classify enemy pieces as undefended, defended once, well defended.
- Prophylaxis: careful with pawn pushes in front of your own king. When losing, skip slow prophylaxis and make every move a problem.
- Openings: do not memorize rigidly; study the middlegame plans the line leads to.
- Endgames: won with pawns, improve the position before pushing, do not rush.
- Why it works: thinking aloud in real games, comparing alternatives constantly. Many 2000+ players name his speedruns as the single biggest help.

### John Bartholomew (Chess Fundamentals, Climbing the Rating Ladder)
- Fundamentals: five videos, undefended pieces, piece coordination, typical mistakes, pawn play, trades, each a live game on that theme.
- Rating Ladder: commentated games against opponents by band.
- Stresses time management and longer time controls. Co-founded Chessable. Known for teaching the Scandinavian.

### Ben Finegold
- Lectures built on stories, humour and catchphrases that make ideas stick. Losing hundreds of games is normal. No written study plan by band.

### Hikaru Nakamura
- Documented advice: do not try to learn everything at once, improve gradually, play lots, work tactical patterns.

### Magnus Carlsen
- His own development: many hours alone replaying games and reading without a board, building patterns of piece placement, pawn structure and plans. Gained about 1,000 points in one year. At 11 his endgames lagged, so he studied an endgame book selectively. Learned from losses to avoid complications when winning.
- Says his strength is intuition built from experience rather than calculation speed.

### Igor Smirnov
- Courses built around diagnosing what holds you back. Always check what the opponent most wants; deal early with enemy pieces in your half; answer a threat with a bigger threat; reduce planning to a few recurring jobs (attack the king, target weaknesses, promote, win pawns).

### Dan Heisman (Novice Nook, The Improving Chess Thinker)
- Hope chess: moving without checking the opponent's forcing replies. Basic hope chess is the beginner version, passive hope chess (1200 to 1700) means glancing and assuming the move is safe. Real chess means checking every candidate for safety every time.
- Five questions each move: what did their move do, what do I want, which moves do it, which are unsafe, which of the safe ones is best. Checks, captures and threats first. Assume the opponent finds their best move.
- His research basis: players from 800 to 2000 thought aloud through the same positions. Stronger players did not mainly calculate deeper; they recognized more kinds of positions.
- Time: use nearly all the clock, more on critical moves.

### Jeremy Silman (imbalances)
- List the imbalances (king safety, material, piece activity, pawn structure, space, files and squares, initiative, development). Each points to a plan.
- Complete Endgame Course is organized by rating band, not topic. Up to 999: basic mates. The next bands add pawn endings, then Lucena and Philidor.

### Artur Yusupov (Build Up Your Chess)
- Nine books, three series by three levels, 24 chapters each, one theme per chapter followed by a scored test. Aimed at about 1500, 1800 and 2100 and hard even there. Not for under 1200.

### Jacob Aagaard
- Three questions for quiet positions: where are the weaknesses, which is my worst piece and how do I improve it, what does my opponent want. The best move often answers more than one.

### Chessbrah (Aman Hambleton, Eric Hansen), Building Habits
- Free beginner series, 1.e4 based, played as if at a set rating with narration, no memorization and no opening theory, new habits introduced as the rating rises. Aman favours system openings for improvers.
- Unverified habit list: knights before bishops, castle early, h3 or h6 against pins, take free pieces, trade when ahead, quiet improving move when unsure.

### Andras Toth
- Under 1200: cut blunders with pattern recognition and puzzles. Over 1200: add principles, calculation and a repertoire. Choose openings that lead to varied pawn structures, avoid rigid systems. Start endgame study from your own games. Weekly split for 3 hours: half calculation, a quarter openings, a quarter endgames.

### Chess Dojo (Kraai, Kavutskiy, Pruess)
- Three pillars: learn from someone above, spar with peers, teach someone below; fewer, longer games, each analysed; a structured checklist with cohort accountability.
- Only classical games count. Rating banded task lists from 0 to 2500: games plus analysis, Polgar puzzles, endgames, openings, model games, sparring positions. A 1200 to 1300 FIDE week targets 15 hours.

### Steps Method (Brunia and van Wijgerden)
- The Dutch six step curriculum from beginner to master, teacher led explanation followed by exercises.

## Consensus
1. Safety first: stop hanging pieces, check every move for safety.
2. Scan forcing moves first: checks, captures, threats.
3. Daily tactics or pattern work.
4. Analyze your own games, especially losses.
5. Short, concrete plans driven by the features of the position rather than memorized lines.
6. Think about the opponent's idea every move.
7. Endgames by level: basic mates first.

## Disagreements
| Issue | One side | The other |
|---|---|---|
| Openings under 1200 | Levy: an opening per colour from day one | Building Habits and the 2000+ survey: principles, no theory below about 1600 |
| Time control | Dojo: classical only | Nakamura, Naroditsky, Chessbrah teach through blitz |
| Systems | Aman: London suits improvers | Toth: avoid rigid systems, see varied structures |
| Process | Heisman: explicit checklist every move | Naroditsky: the scan at critical moments; Carlsen: intuition from experience |
| Delivery | Entertainment first (Levy, Chessbrah, Finegold) | Structured homework (Yusupov, Dojo, Steps) |

## What makes the coaching effective
Thinking aloud in real games, drills and repetition, checklists that apply in any position, self testing with scores, peers and accountability, stories that make ideas stick.
